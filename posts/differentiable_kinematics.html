Differentiable kinematics, Jacobians, and Jacobian transpose

January 1st, 2026

Overview

We cover forward kinematics and the Jacobian, relating joint velocities to end-effector velocities with the Jacobian, and forces to torques via the Jacobian transpose. All vectors are assumed to be column vectors, transposes are written explicitly $(\cdot)^{T}$, and we refer to "task space" and "Cartesian space" interchangeably.

Forward Kinematics

We model the joint configuration of the robot at time $t$ as

$$ q(t) : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n $$

where $n$ is the number of joints / number of degrees of freedom (DoF). We denote the task space of the robot (for example, the end-effector position and orientation) at time $t$ as

$$ x(t) : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m $$

where $m$ is the task-space / Cartesian space dimensionality. In this post we focus on end-effector position; orientation can be included by extending the task-space vector and Jacobian.

The kinematic structure of the robot determines a forward kinematics function $F$

$$ F: \mathbb{R}^{n} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m $$

which maps joint configurations to the task space:

$$ x(t) = F(q(t)) $$

Differentiable Kinematics and the Jacobian

If the forward kinematics function is differentiable with respect to $q$, we can differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to time. Applying the chain rule yields a relationship between joint velocities and Cartesian velocities:

$$ \begin{aligned} x(t) &= F(q(t)) \\ \frac{dx}{dt}(t) &= \frac{dF(q(t))}{dt} = \frac{dF}{dq}(q(t)) \frac{dq}{dt}(t) \\ \dot{x}(t) &= \frac{dF}{dq}(q(t)) \dot{q}(t) \end{aligned} $$ Where we define the Cartesian and joint velocities as: $$ \begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t) &:= \frac{dx}{dt}(t) \\ \dot{q}(t) &:= \frac{dq}{dt}(t) \end{aligned} $$

and the Jacobian matrix as

$$ \begin{aligned} J(q):=\frac{dF}{dq}(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \end{aligned} $$

Which lets us rewrite the above differentiable kinematics equations as

$$ \begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t) &=J(q(t)) \dot{q}(t) \end{aligned} $$

The Jacobian maps joint velocities to Cartesian velocities and depends explicitly on the current joint configuration.

Jacobian Transpose relates forces and torques

Torques and forces are also related via the transpose of the Jacobian. To derive this, remember that power $P$ (measured in joules per second) is the rate of energy transfer. Power can be expressed in Cartesian space:

$$ P_{\text{Cartesian}} = F^{T} \dot{x} $$

where $F \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ is a constant force vector applied to the end effector, and $\dot{x}$ is the Cartesian velocity as defined above. Notice that $F$ and $\dot{x}$ are both (column) vectors, and since we are taking their dot product, we transpose the force vector $F^{T}$.

Power can also be expressed in the configuration space, where we replace force $F$ and Cartesian velocity $\dot{x}$, with their joint analogues torque $\tau \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and joint velocity $\dot{q}$ respectively (the latter defined above).

$$ P_{\text{joint}} = \tau^{T} \dot{q} $$

Since $\tau$ and $\dot{q}$ are both (column) vectors and we are taking their dot product, we also transpose the torque $\tau$ as above for force.

Since total power is conserved between Cartesian and joint spaces, we can equal these together, and then rearrange the equation to relate torque $\tau$ to forces $F$ via the transpose of the Jacobian $J^{T}$.

$$ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{Cartesian}} &= P_{\text{joint}} &\quad& \text{(equal power)} \\ F^T \dot{x} &= \tau^{T} \dot{q} &\quad& \text{(substitute power definitions)} \\ F^T J \dot{q} &= \tau^{T} \dot{q} &\quad& \text{(since } \dot{x} = J \dot{q}\text{)} \\ F^T J &= \tau^{T} &\quad& \text{(holds for all } \dot{q}\text{)} \\ J^{T} F &= \tau &\quad& \text{(transpose both sides)} \end{aligned} $$

Notice how our final relationship $J^{T} F = \tau$ has the force $F$ and torque $\tau$ vectors both as column vectors, as expected.

2 DoF Revolute Arm Example

In practice, $F$ is often nonlinear and can be quite complex, depending on the robot’s geometry. For example, consider a serial robot arm composed of two links, with lengths $l_{0}$ and $l_{1}$. The first link is connected to the world via a revolute joint, and the second link is connected to the end of the first link via a revolute joint.

The end-effectors 2D position $x$ in the plane is given by:

$$ \begin{aligned} x = F(q) &= F([\theta_{0}, \theta_{1}]) = \begin{pmatrix} l_0 \cos(\theta_0) + l_1 \cos(\theta_0 + \theta_1) \\ l_0 \sin(\theta_0) + l_1 \sin(\theta_0 + \theta_1) \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

The Jacobian is obtained by differentiating the forward kinematics with respect to the joint angles:

$$ J(q) = \frac{\partial x}{\partial q} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_0} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_1} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_0} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_1} \end{pmatrix} $$

As a result, we know that the Jacobian for this robot arm is:

$$ J(q) = J([\theta_{0}, \theta_{1}]) = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin(\theta_{0})\,l_{0} - \sin(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})\,l_{1} & -\sin(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})\,l_{1} \\ \cos(\theta_{0})\,l_{0} + \cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})\,l_{1} & \cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})\,l_{1} \end{pmatrix} $$

If a Cartesian force \(F = [F_x, F_y]^T\) is applied at the end effector, the corresponding joint torques are:

$$ \tau = J^T(q)\,F $$
