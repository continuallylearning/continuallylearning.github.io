Centripetal Acceleration

November 9th, 2025

Centripetal acceleration is the inward acceleration a body undergoes during uniform 2D circular motion. We can derive it kinematically by taking the derivative of $p(t)$ twice with respect to time, which gives the acceleration. By multiplying this acceleration by mass, we obtain the centripetal force.

Position in uniform 2D circular motion

Consider a body moving in uniform, 2D circular motion. The position $p(t) : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ at time $t$ can be expressed as:

$$ p(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r \cos(\theta(t)) \\ r \sin(\theta(t)) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r \cos(\omega t) \\ r \sin(\omega t) \end{bmatrix} $$ where \[ \theta(t) = \omega t, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f \]

Here, $\theta(t)$ represents the angular position, and $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the body. Since the motion is uniform, $\omega$ is constant. If the body completes rotations at frequency $f$, then $\omega = 2\pi f$.

Linear velocity in uniform 2D circular motion

Now that we've defined position, let's compute the first derivative of $p(t)$ with respect to $t$, which is the linear velocity $v(t)$. Using the chain rule:

$$ v(t) = \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -r\omega \sin(\omega t) \\ r\omega \cos(\omega t) \end{bmatrix} = \omega \begin{bmatrix} -y(t) \\ x(t) \end{bmatrix} $$

Since the magnitude of $p(t)$ is always $r$, we see that the magnitude of $v(t)$ is $\omega r$, and that the velocity vector is perpendicular to the radius (and therefore tangent to the circle).

Centripetal acceleration in uniform 2D circular motion

Taking the derivative of $v(t)$ with respect to time:

$$ a(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -r\omega^2 \cos(\omega t) \\ -r\omega^2 \sin(\omega t) \end{bmatrix} = \omega^2 \begin{bmatrix} -x(t) \\ -y(t) \end{bmatrix} = -\omega^2 p(t) $$

We have shown that the centripetal acceleration points exactly opposite the position vector (i.e., toward the center), and its magnitude is $\omega^2 r$. Since $\omega = \frac{v}{r}$, we have

$$ a(t) = -\omega^2 p(t) = -\omega^2 r\,\hat{p}(t) = -\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^2 r\,\hat{p}(t) = -\frac{v^2}{r}\,\hat{p}(t) $$

where $\hat{p}(t)$ is the unit vector in the direction of $p(t)$ (i.e., $p(t) = r \hat{p}(t)$). We have now shown both the direction of centripetal acceleration (inward and perpendicular to the velocity) and its magnitude $\frac{v^2}{r}$. The direction makes sense: if the acceleration were not perpendicular to the velocity, the speed would change, contradicting the assumption of uniform circular motion.
