Centripetal Acceleration

November 9th, 2025

Centripetal acceleration is inward acceleration the body undergoes during the uniform, circular 2D motion. We can derive it kinematically by taking the derivative of $p(t)$ twice with respect to time, which gives us the acceleration. By multiplying the resulting acceleration by mass, we can calculate the centripedal force.

Position in uniform, 2D circular motion

Consider a body moving in uniform, 2D circular motion. By 2D circular motion, we mean that the position $p(t) : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2 $ of the body at time $t$ can expressed as: $$ p(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} rcos(\theta(t)) \\ rsin(\theta(t)) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} rcos(\omega t) \\ rsin(\omega t) \end{bmatrix} $$ where \begin{aligned} \theta(t) = \omega t \\ \omega = 2\pi f \end{aligned} Here, $\theta(t)$ represents the angle in the circle where the body is at time $t$, and $\omega$ is the magnitude of angular velocity of the body. Since we assume the circular motion is uniform, we assume $\omega$ is a constant with respect to time. More specifically, if the body rotates around the circle at a certain frequency $f$, then we can say $\omega = 2\pi f$ to relate frequency to angular velocity, and express the amount of radians per unit time the body rotates around the circle.

Linear velocity in uniform, 2D circular motion

Now that we've defined position, lets calculate the first derivate of $p(t)$ with respect $t$, which is the linear velocity $v(t)$. Note that we'll have to apply the chain rule: $$ v(t) = \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t}(t)\\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}(t)\\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -r\omega sin(\omega t) \\ r\omega cos(\omega t)\\ \end{bmatrix} = \omega \begin{bmatrix} -y(t) \\ x(t)\\ \end{bmatrix} $$ Since the magnitude of $p(t)$ must always equal $r$ (due to circular motion), we have actually just shown that the magnitude of $v = \omega r$ and that the linear velocity is pointing in a direction perpendicular to the direction towards the circle's center.

Centripedal acceleration in uniform, 2D circular motion

Let's take the derivative of the linear velocity with respect to time: $$ a(t) = \frac{\partial^{2} p}{\partial t^{2}}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 x}{\partial t^2}(t)\\ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}(t)\\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -r\omega^2 cos(\omega t) \\ -r\omega^2 sin(\omega t)\\ \end{bmatrix} = \omega^2 \begin{bmatrix} -x(t) \\ -y(t)\\ \end{bmatrix} = -\omega^2 p(t) $$ We have just shown that the centripedal acceleration points in the exact opposite direction of the position vector (i.e: the acceleration is always inwards towards the circle center and therefore perpendicular to the linear velocity), and it's magnitude is $\omega^{2}*r$ (since $p(t)$ has magnitude $r$). Since $\omega = \frac{v}{r}$, we can see That $$ a = -\omega^2 p(t) = -\omega^2 r\hat{p}(t) = - (\frac{v}{r})^2 r\hat(p)(t) = - \frac{v^2}{r} \hat{p}(t) $$ Where $\hat{p}(t)$ is the unit vector in direction $p(t)$ (i.e: p(t) = r * \hat{p}(t)). We have now shown the direction of centripetal acceleration (towards the center and always perpendicular to linear velocity) and its magnitude ($\frac{v^2}{r}). The direction of centripetal acceleration makes sense since if it wasn't perpendicular to the linear velocity at all times, then there would be acceleration in the linear velocity direction, which would violate the uniform circular motion assumption.
